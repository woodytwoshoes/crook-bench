DR: Daniel, here's where I've landed. Everything I've found points to a muscular strain of the lower back — what we call acute non-specific or mechanical low back pain. The muscles beside your spine on the left are tight and tender, which is why you're leaning slightly and bending hurts. Importantly, the nerves to your legs are working perfectly: your strength, reflexes and feeling are all normal, the nerve stretch tests are negative, and there are no warning signs like leg pain below the knee, bladder or bowel trouble, fever, or night pain. That means I'm not worried about a slipped disc pressing on a nerve, a fracture, infection or anything sinister.

About the MRI: I know it feels like the obvious next step, but with your picture it genuinely wouldn't help, and it can actually make things worse. Scans in people your age very commonly show "bulging discs" and wear-and-tear that are completely normal and present in people with no pain at all — and seeing those words on a report tends to make people more anxious, more cautious, and slower to recover. Guidelines here and worldwide say no imaging in the first 6 weeks unless there are warning signs, and you have none. Surgery is only for the small group with nerve compression that isn't settling, which isn't you. If things change — or you're not improving in about 4–6 weeks — we revisit imaging then.

The good news is that this kind of back strain has an excellent outlook: most people are much better within 2 weeks and back to normal in about 6. The single most important thing is to keep moving. Lying on the couch feels protective but actually slows recovery and stiffens things up — the back is strong and it's safe to use it even when it hurts. So: short walks several times a day, gradually getting longer; sit for shorter periods and get up and move every 30 minutes; avoid heavy lifting for a couple of weeks but otherwise go about your normal activities, including work. Heat packs are fine and are helping you, so keep using them.

For pain relief, I'll give you ibuprofen 400 mg three times a day with food for up to 5 days — anti-inflammatories work better than paracetamol for this. Take it regularly for the first few days rather than waiting for the pain to build, and stop if you get stomach upset. You can take paracetamol alongside it if needed. I'm deliberately not giving you strong painkillers like opioids — they don't work well for back pain, they have side effects, and you've already had a bad reaction to tramadol.

I've also referred you to a physiotherapist, who can give you targeted exercises, help you get moving confidently, and address anything about your desk setup — especially with your deadline, working from a proper chair and desk rather than the couch will help.

Please come straight back, or go to the emergency department if it's after hours, if you develop any of these: numbness around your back passage or genitals, difficulty passing urine or losing control of bladder or bowels, weakness or numbness in a leg, pain shooting down below the knee, fever, or pain that becomes severe at night or at rest. Those are the warning signs that would change the plan and need urgent assessment.

I'd like to see you again in 2 weeks to check you're on track — sooner if you're concerned. Does that plan make sense, and do you have any questions?
