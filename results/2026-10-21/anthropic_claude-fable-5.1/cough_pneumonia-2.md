DR: Mark, here's what I've found. Your temperature is 38, your breathing is a little fast, and your oxygen level is 95%, which is acceptable. When I listened to and tapped on your chest, the lower part of your right lung sounds and feels different from the left, and the chest X-ray confirms it: you have pneumonia, an infection in the lower right lung. The rusty phlegm, the shaking chills and the sharp pain when you breathe in all fit with this. The good news is that it's a mild pneumonia on all the measures we use: your blood pressure, oxygen, alertness and age all put you in the group that can be safely treated at home with tablets rather than in hospital.

The plan:
1. Antibiotics: amoxicillin, 2 capsules (1 gram) three times a day for 7 days. Finish the whole course even once you feel better. This is the recommended first-choice antibiotic for this type of pneumonia. Side effects can include loose bowels or a rash; if you get a rash, swelling of the face or lips, or trouble breathing, stop it and seek help straight away.
2. Paracetamol, 2 tablets up to four times a day (no more than 8 tablets in 24 hours) for the fever and the chest pain. Taking the edge off the pain is important so you can breathe deeply and cough the phlegm up, which helps clear the infection.
3. Rest, drink plenty of fluids, and stay off work for at least this week. I'll give you a medical certificate. Don't suppress the cough with cough medicines; coughing is doing a job.
4. I've taken a nose swab for flu and COVID; the result comes back tomorrow, and if it shows flu I'll call you because there's a specific treatment for that. In the meantime, keep your distance from others, cover coughs and wash your hands.
5. I want to see you again in 48 hours, so the day after tomorrow, to check you're turning the corner. If you're not clearly improving by then, we'd add a second antibiotic that covers other types of germs. If you're not getting better, that visit is essential, not optional.

Now, the important warning signs. Go to the emergency department, or call 000 for an ambulance, if any of these happen: you become breathless at rest or struggle to speak in full sentences; your lips or fingers look blue or grey; the chest pain becomes severe or constant; you cough up actual blood; you become confused, very drowsy, or hard to rouse; you can't keep fluids down or stop passing urine; or you have a very high fever with shaking that doesn't settle with paracetamol. Also, if you feel you're going downhill at any time rather than holding steady, don't wait for the appointment.

Longer term, we should repeat your chest X-ray in about six weeks to make sure the lung has fully cleared, and I'd recommend you get the flu vaccine each year from now on. Does that all make sense, and do you have any questions?
