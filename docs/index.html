Crook bench

How AI models do as the GP in a consultation game

Each model plays the doctor in the five free cases of Crook, an Australian general-practice game, three times each. It talks to an AI patient in its own words, examines, orders tests, prescribes and refers through tools, then commits to a diagnosis. The game's own code scores every consultation against a hand-written key, exactly as it scores a human player. A score here measures play of a game, not fitness to practise medicine.

      Method and limits

      The doctor is the model under test. It acts only through tools: say to the patient, list_exams and search to find exams, tests, drugs, referrals and diagnoses, then examine, order_test, prescribe, refer, diagnose and end_consult. It sees what a human player sees: the patient's name, age and booking, the clinic record, and its own findings and results. It never sees the case file or the points.

      The patient is the game's own patient model, playing a person from a private chart; it does not know its diagnosis. A separate classifier call decides which history items each question asks about, and only those facts are released to the patient. A question it misses gets a denial, as it would for a human player.

      The score is decided by code against a hand-written key: red flags asked about, key history, indicated exams and tests, a safe plan and a defensible diagnosis. It scores the process, not the outcome. Unnecessary tests cost points, more for each further one, and harmful actions cost a lot. Spoken advice is judged at the end from the doctor's own words, with code checking that each quote was really said.

      The budget is 50 questions a consultation. Explanations, the plan, examining, tests and prescriptions are free.

      The GP registrar is one of the paper's authors, a practising GP registrar in Australia. They played each free case once on the live game, briskly (about four questions a consultation), and their consultations were rescored under the 21 October rules. Five consultations by one person are a reference point, not a human baseline.

      Limits

      • All five cases are drafts written by the game's author, a GP registrar, from standard references, and follow Australian practice (eTG, PBS).
      • The patient, the classifier and the judge are small language models. They miss some questions and sometimes credit or miss a spoken item. Every doctor faces the same patient, but the noise is real. On 21 October the patient was a Gemini model, and Gemini 4 Argon was among the doctors.
      • Three consultations a case is a small sample; the ± is one standard deviation of a doctor's consultations.
      • It is a benchmark of a game. It says nothing about whether any model can or should practise medicine.
